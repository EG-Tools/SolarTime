VISUAL MODEL · SIZE AND DISTANCE

Why one view cannot show every true scale

Planets are tiny compared with the distances between them. A screen that preserves both exactly makes most bodies vanish, so Solar Time offers a readable overview and an actual-size comparison rather than pretending one scale can do every job.

Written by Life UserPublished 2026-10-16Reviewed 2026-10-16

The physical ratio is larger than a viewport

Earth's diameter is about 12,742 kilometres, while its average distance from the Sun is about 149.6 million kilometres. If Earth were drawn as a two-pixel disc, the corresponding Sun–Earth distance would extend far beyond an ordinary display. Making the orbit fit pushes Earth below one pixel, and including Neptune makes the mismatch stronger.

Textbooks and interactive models therefore separate body scale from distance scale. The important requirement is clarity: identify the adjusted view, provide a way to compare it with a more physical ratio, and avoid presenting decorative spacing as a precise measurement.

Normal view is a comparative diagram

Solar Time's normal view enlarges bodies and manages orbit gaps so the Sun, planets, and major moons remain selectable. It is useful for relative ordering, phases, composition, and parent–moon relationships. It is not a ruler, and a pixel gap in this mode should not be converted directly into kilometres.

Individual size controls also belong to this presentation layer. Enlarging the Moon or Europa makes surface detail and eclipse demonstrations easier to see without changing the astronomical radius stored by the model.

Actual-size mode exposes the disparity

Actual-size mode reduces artificial enlargement and moves spacing toward physical relationships. “Actual size” describes the ratio used by the visualization; it does not mean a monitor contains a literal kilometre-scale system. Projection, field of view, clipping limits, and minimum pixels still matter.

At maximum camera distance, a physically projected planet can cover less than one pixel. Solar Time then uses a point-like representation. The Sun has the largest minimum, giant planets a smaller tier, rocky planets another tier, and moons plus Pluto the smallest. This hierarchy preserves discovery without making every body equally bright.

LOD should change detail, not apparent size

A textured sphere is useful nearby but wasteful when it covers a fraction of a pixel. A point sprite is efficient far away. During the handoff, coverage and brightness overlap smoothly. Otherwise the body can collapse and then grow, or appear to flash when both forms are fully visible.

Lighting must participate too. A backlit sphere may be dark while a navigation point is bright. If the point ignores the sphere's visual state, approaching the body looks like a lamp suddenly switched off.

Interpretation rule: use normal view for comparison and interaction; use actual-size mode to appreciate scale disparity. Use numerical data when measurement matters.

Try it in Solar Time

  1. Press 0 and switch actual-size mode on and off.
  2. Set orbit spacing to 100% and move as far back as possible. Identify the different minimum point tiers.
  3. Move slowly toward a selected planet and watch the point become a texture without a size jump.
  4. Repeat with a backlit body and compare the brightness transition.
