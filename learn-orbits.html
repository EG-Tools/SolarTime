MODEL · TIME AND MOTION

From clock time to planetary motion

A clock supplies one instant. A solar-system view needs positions, parent–child relationships, display transforms, and a camera. Solar Time builds those layers in sequence instead of playing a recorded animation.

Written by Life UserPublished 2026-10-16Reviewed 2026-10-17

One time value drives the scene

Real-time mode and manual playback are two ways of choosing the same input: an instant. Real time continually replaces that instant with the current clock. Manual playback advances it by the selected number of hours, days, or years per second. Pausing keeps the chosen instant fixed, allowing the camera to move without the bodies continuing along their orbits.

Returning a speed slider to zero therefore stops at the simulated date already reached. It does not jump back to the present. Returning to real time is a separate action because it changes the selected instant, not merely the playback rate.

Positions are calculated, not pre-rendered

Solar Time evaluates positions for the selected time. Modern-era planetary positions use established astronomical formulae; distant dates use bounded approximations that favor stable exploration over observatory precision. Elliptical motion also means a planet does not travel at a perfectly constant angular speed: it moves faster near perihelion and slower near aphelion.

The displayed orbit may be widened or compressed, but the time-dependent orbital phase remains the starting point. This separation lets the app offer a readable overview without claiming that every screen distance is a physical measurement.

Moons are solved relative to their parents

The Moon belongs to Earth's local system, Europa to Jupiter's, and Triton to Neptune's. Their local positions are combined with the parent planet's solar position. This hierarchy explains why changing a satellite orbit control should alter the local Earth–Moon, Jupiter–Europa, or Neptune–Triton separation without rearranging unrelated planets.

Mercury is different: it orbits the Sun as a planet. A local Sun–Mercury spacing control can prevent visual overlap, while the global orbit-spacing control still governs the planetary layout as a whole.

Triton makes orbital direction visible

Triton is Neptune's largest moon and moves around Neptune opposite to the planet's rotation: a retrograde orbit. NASA's Triton overview explains this unusual motion. Solar Time uses JPL NEP097 mean orbital elements with nodal precession rather than a full numerical ephemeris.

Open Neptune's card, enable Triton, then select the moon in the footer and follow it. Advance simulation time to compare its orbit with Neptune. Both normal and true-size views keep the satellite orbit tilted, while normal view enlarges its body and spacing for readability. Its initial displayed size is comparable to Pluto; true-size mode uses Triton's larger physical radius. These presentation choices do not change its orbital direction.

The surface texture completes gaps in the Voyager imagery artistically. It is useful for visual exploration, but the added terrain is not an observation; see the source and reconstruction credits.

Astronomical and displayed states stay separate

The default overview enlarges bodies and manages gaps so several objects remain selectable. Actual-size mode reduces those adjustments. At extreme distance, the browser still needs minimum visible points so tiny bodies do not disappear entirely. Those points are presentation aids; they do not change the stored planetary radius or orbit.

Accuracy boundary: Solar Time omits some long-period perturbations and does not provide the uncertainty data required for navigation, occultation prediction, or professional eclipse timing.

Try it in Solar Time

  1. Press 0, pause with Space, and move the camera. Body positions should remain tied to the paused instant.
  2. Turn real time off, select days, and advance the simulation. Compare the motion of inner and outer planets.
  3. Select Earth and then the Moon. Change only the Moon orbit control and watch the local separation.
  4. Switch between normal and actual-size views and identify which changes belong to presentation.
